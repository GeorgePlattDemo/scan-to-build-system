The research did not find one “angled outdoor table.” It found separate geometric systems.

The same finished category can be produced by 10°, 23°, 25°, 30°, 60°, 7°, X-segment, crossed-leg and multi-bevel systems. That is precisely why the family boundary must precede any future configurator.

Commercial evidence and cut-geometry evidence do different jobs.
Pilot Rock is strong on overall product dimensions, material system and hardware but does not publish the A-frame leg angle/length. Dura-Trel publishes unusually strong part lengths and connection topology but still does not publish the crossed-leg angle. Those sources remain useful precisely because the missing geometry is recorded as UNKNOWN, not reverse-engineered from pictures.
Angle pedigree is not just a number.
The best public plans distinguish 10° off square, 10° double bevel, 30° with parallel ends, 30° with non-parallel ends, long-point-to-short-point, long-point-to-long-point, and even different angles at opposite ends of one member. A future fact model needs those fields separately.

07

Family dossiers

These dossiers describe the geometry that defines each construction family, what a customer would actually measure, what may become bounded, and what should remain frozen or deferred.

1

ATTACHED A-FRAME

Two splayed leg pairs support one table-and-attached-seat system. The useful invariant is not 'picnic table'; it is the side-frame station: leg angle/end convention, tabletop datum, seat datum, cross support, bracing and connections.

ANGLE / DEPENDENCY

  • Legs carry the principal splay; diagonal/angle braces may carry additional angles.
  • Home Depot, TOH, Ana White and Construct101 prove that 10°, 23°, 25° and 30° systems can all produce recognizable A-frames without being interchangeable.

WHAT A CUSTOMER WOULD MEASURE

  • Overall table height
  • seat height
  • leg member length with its measurement edge/datum
  • leg angle/end convention
  • top/seat support positions
  • actual connection/hole locations for repair

REASONABLE CONFIGURATION

  • Longitudinal top/seat length can often be separated from a frozen end station.
  • A verified source-specific station width/height parameter might later become bounded if a complete dependency model is established.

DO NOT FREELY CONFIGURE

  • Leg angle, brace system, cross-brace height, bolt pattern and seat/table relationship must not become independent sliders merely because they are easy to draw.

Evidence usually missing: Most public DIY sources publish cuts but not reusable hole-center coordinates. Commercial products often publish overall size and hardware but withhold leg cut geometry.

Engineering boundary: Changing side-frame angle, foot spread, brace position, cross-brace relation or connection pattern changes the structural system and requires new authority.

2

DETACHED ANGLED / TRESTLE TABLE

Independent table with splayed/sawhorse/trestle end frames tied by stretchers/aprons. It is geometrically separate from any benches placed beside it.

ANGLE / DEPENDENCY

  • Splayed legs, lower supports, diagonal braces and/or trestle posts carry the angle.
  • A longitudinal stretcher commonly ties two end stations; its end position is a dependency, not decoration.

WHAT A CUSTOMER WOULD MEASURE

  • Tabletop datum/size
  • end-frame height and width/foot spread
  • leg member length and angle
  • support/rail positions
  • stretcher height/length
  • brace endpoints

REASONABLE CONFIGURATION

  • Longitudinal top length is often an independent bounded input when the end station remains frozen.
  • Some end-frame width changes may be derivable only after a source-specific triangle/connection model exists.

DO NOT FREELY CONFIGURE

  • Do not independently change leg angle, top height, foot spread and stretcher height without solving the same end frame.

Evidence usually missing: Exact angle datum and bolt/half-lap coordinates are often trapped in diagrams or omitted from commercial sheets.

Engineering boundary: Changing splay or member intersections changes load paths and connection geometry; defer until the family has a verified transform.

3

X-FRAME TABLE

End frames contain crossing members or segmented X supports whose intersection is a defining datum. 'Looks like an X' is not enough; the crossing location and end conditions define the family.

ANGLE / DEPENDENCY

  • The X members carry the angle; some sources split the X into long/short segments, others use continuous 4×4 members with laps.
  • Stretchers often land at X centers or tie end frames at a fixed datum.

WHAT A CUSTOMER WOULD MEASURE

  • Finished end-frame height/width
  • member length
  • each end angle
  • crossing point
  • lap/bolt geometry
  • stretcher centerline
  • top support datum

REASONABLE CONFIGURATION

  • Tabletop length can usually vary while a validated X end station remains frozen.
  • End-frame width/height may be parameterizable only if crossing and end cuts are solved together.

DO NOT FREELY CONFIGURE

  • Never let X width, X height, crossing point and member angles drift independently.

Evidence usually missing: Many aesthetically strong X-table plans publish drawings but not a text-readable crossing coordinate or lap dimensions.

Engineering boundary: Moving the crossing, changing member section, or relocating stretchers changes the structural relationship; source-specific validation or engineering is required.

4

DETACHED ANGLED BENCH

Separate bench with splayed legs, A-frame legs or angled side-frame members supporting the seat. Seat geometry and leg/support geometry are separate fact groups.

ANGLE / DEPENDENCY

  • Legs and back/support members may carry different angles; some bench designs combine seat-support and backrest angles.
  • Longitudinal seat boards can often be dimensionally independent from the end frame.

WHAT A CUSTOMER WOULD MEASURE

  • Seat height
  • seat depth
  • seat length
  • leg/member length
  • foot spread
  • support height
  • back angle separately from structural leg angle
  • connection positions

REASONABLE CONFIGURATION

  • Seat length is commonly a clean bounded input if the end stations remain frozen.
  • Repair paths can prioritize the matching good side/leg as project evidence.

DO NOT FREELY CONFIGURE

  • Do not let ergonomic backrest angle stand in for leg splay or support angle. Do not infer a generic bolt pattern.

Evidence usually missing: Connection offsets and part datums are frequently missing even when the angle and length are published.

Engineering boundary: Changing leg splay, side-frame support location, or back/seat load relationships requires source-specific authority.

5

X-FRAME BENCH

Bench end or side frames are built from crossing/segmented members. The X crossing is part of the definition and often controls seat datum and foot spread.

ANGLE / DEPENDENCY

  • X members carry the angle; support/seat members fix the X relative to the seating surface.
  • Segmented-X plans may have multiple part classes with parallel and non-parallel cuts.

WHAT A CUSTOMER WOULD MEASURE

  • Seat datum/height
  • end-frame width
  • X segment/member lengths
  • end-angle convention
  • crossing location
  • center support/stretcher
  • connection method

REASONABLE CONFIGURATION

  • Seat length can be a clean bounded edit with frozen X stations.
  • A source-specific X station could later expose one controlled dimension if all dependent segment lengths/intersections are derived from one fact model.

DO NOT FREELY CONFIGURE

  • Do not represent X-frame height, width, crossing and member angle as four independent knobs.

Evidence usually missing: Continuous-X designs often omit cut geometry; segmented-X DIY plans can be much more complete.

Engineering boundary: Changing X geometry changes the intersection and load path; absent a verified transform, DEFER.

6

OTHER USEFUL ANGLED FAMILY

Two especially useful research classes emerged: convertible table↔bench systems with pivot geometry, and multi-angle sawhorse/modern benches where miter versus bevel is itself part identity.

ANGLE / DEPENDENCY

  • Convertible systems add a kinematic datum (pivot) to ordinary angle geometry.
  • Multi-angle designs may use different angles at opposite ends of one part, making 'angle = 10°' inadequate.

WHAT A CUSTOMER WOULD MEASURE

  • Pivot center and hardware
  • clearance envelope across states
  • each end-cut convention
  • member measurement edge
  • state-specific datums
  • material section

REASONABLE CONFIGURATION

  • A frozen mechanism may permit limited longitudinal sizing if pivots/end stations remain fixed.
  • Angle-rich small-part sets are good laboratories for provenance-aware part definitions.

DO NOT FREELY CONFIGURE

  • Do not treat a moving mechanism as two unrelated static configurations. Do not collapse bevel/miter/off-square into one generic angle field.

Evidence usually missing: Pivot coordinates and complete motion envelopes are often in drawings but not text. Compound-saw conventions may be described inconsistently across publishers.

Engineering boundary: Changing pivot, mechanism geometry, or complex brace relationships crosses into kinematic/structural authority.

10

Research conclusion

The evidence supports a narrower and more useful definition of Outdoor Build than “table / bench / picnic table.” The high-value unit is a named geometric station: a sourced family with member identities, angle conventions, datums, intersections and dependencies. Longitudinal sizing is often separable. End-frame geometry usually is not.

A future user should be able to click one angled member and answer five questions:
WHAT IS THIS? · WHERE DO I MEASURE IT FROM? · WHAT ANGLE IS THAT? · WHAT DOES IT CONNECT TO? · WHAT MOVES IF I CHANGE THIS?

If the source set cannot answer one of those questions, the corresponding fact remains project evidence, source-specific work, deferred, or unknown. It does not become a convenience default.
