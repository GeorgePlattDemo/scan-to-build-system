# 13 — Frontier work by others

*Status: research-positioning note. The cited works are adjacent materials. They do not validate a deployed Scan-to-Build service. None of these authors has seen this document.*

**Not authored here.** This item collects published work by other researchers. None of it validates or evaluates the architecture in items 1–12, and none of these authors has seen this document.

The question runs from one project, through a local business, out to workforce and place. Readers from different fields will find their own end of it. The works below come from fabrication robotics, wood-products manufacturing, supply-chain theory, information architecture, workforce standards, and regional economics.

Fifteen research works and one skill standard. Five get full treatment, because the question in this package meets them head on. Six get a short paragraph — they establish that something holds, but the question is not decided there. Four appear at the end as lineage. People have worked this ground for decades, and none of it should be mistaken for new.

Workflow: scan → define → select → confirm → cut · mill · drill → label → pickup → install

Every work below is measured against that sequence. Their paths differ. The sequence does not.

---

## A. Adapting to the material

Work in this group moves capability toward the wood. Uncertainty in the material is treated as something to sense and accommodate during fabrication.

### 1. Mallegol, Rohart and Champroy (2026) — *The tree dictates the shape: reversing the industrial paradigm in rural areas*

**Question.** How can irregular, small-diameter timber become usable material when centralized industrial processes do not fit its geometry?

**Answer.** The study examines adaptive fabrication close to the material source, treating variation in the material as a design input rather than only as a defect. It identifies the programming burden associated with variable geometry as a practical barrier to transfer, and notes the absence of a commercial software solution for that task.

**Open boundary.** The case concerns material whose geometry is irregular. It does not examine ordinary graded dimensional lumber and sheet goods already held at a dealer yard.

**Where it meets the sequence.** The study moves process because the material will not remain standardized. The sequence asks what changes when stock is already standardized and project purpose arrives resolved before processing begins.

**Research question at the seam.** If ordinary stock already sits still and project purpose arrives resolved, how small can the adjacent fabrication increment remain?

### 2. Adel, Ruan, McGee and Mozaffari (2024) — *Feedback-driven adaptive multi-robot timber construction*

**Question.** How should fabrication respond when ordinary dimensional lumber deviates from its nominal digital model?

**Answer.** The authors address real deviation in dimensional lumber — material imperfections, calibration between robots, and fabrication inaccuracy. They develop pose-based and topology-based adaptive fabrication methods, incorporating perception, reasoning and action, and evaluate them through building-scale experiments: a nail-laminated timber panel and a full-scale timber wall frame, measuring deviation between the as-built scan and the as-planned model.

**Open boundary.** The work resolves material uncertainty during execution, through sensing and correction. It does not examine resolving customer and site uncertainty before commitment, nor a declared envelope outside which work is refused rather than adapted.

**Where it meets the sequence.** Both approaches confront the fact that wood is not its nominal dimension. They locate the response at opposite ends: this work adapts at the machine, the sequence resolves at definition and declines what falls outside.

**Research question at the seam.** Which uncertainty is better resolved before fabrication, and which is better handled by sensing and adaptation during it? This is a design trade-off rather than a settled question, and neither position obviously prevails.

### 3. Nahmad Vazquez, Garivani and Dackiw (2024) — *Decentralized, data-informed, robotic-based digital timber micro-factories*

**Question.** Can timber fabrication move toward local material, local skill, and distributed production settings?

**Answer.** The paper presents a decentralized, data-informed robotic timber micro-factory model, examining modular production capability located closer to local material and location-specific conditions.

**Open boundary.** The work does not focus on dealer-held inventory, a narrow fixed operation set, or a local business's explicit authority to accept or refuse each project.

**Where it meets the sequence.** Both approaches move fabrication closer to material and use digital information to prepare work before fabrication begins.

**Research question at the seam.** Can a smaller fixed-tool cell operate beside ordinary dealer-held lumber and sheet goods when the accepted project class is deliberately narrow?

## B. Responding to demand

Work in this group starts from the other end. Firms working ordinary stock face customization pressure, and the question is how the supply chain and the firm reorganize in response.

### 4. Nicholls and Bumgardner (2018) — *Challenges and opportunities for North American hardwood manufacturers to adopt customization strategies in an era of increased competition*

**Question.** How should North American hardwood manufacturers respond to international competition and changing customer demand?

**Answer.** The authors, writing from the USDA Forest Service, argue that customization can create competitive advantage, that it requires technology, training, and appropriately configured lean or agile production, and that local sourcing can shorten lead times. They discuss mass customization as individually configured products produced with flexible, responsive manufacturing near mass-production economics — and observe that increasing customer demand for services can push additional service requirements back upstream toward hardwood lumber suppliers and sawmills.

**Open boundary.** The paper follows customization pressure upstream through the hardwood value chain. It does not consider the inverse: resolved demand information traveling upstream while the material remains where it is already held.

**Where it meets the sequence.** The sequence proposes that the information required to satisfy a customized order can move ahead of the material, so that the transformation occurs beside stock already in place.

**Research question at the seam.** If North American hardwood supply chains must become more responsive to changing consumer demand, how much of that responsiveness can be supplied by better-resolved demand information and a bounded processing increment beside existing inventory, rather than by reorganizing the manufacturing chain?

### 5. Kühle, Teischinger and Gronalt (2019) — *Form-based postponement in the solid hardwood supply network*

**Question.** Can specification in a solid-hardwood supply network move later while retaining operational feasibility?

**Answer.** The authors examine downstream finishing and additional decoupling points as means of postponing specification in a solid-hardwood network. They show that later specification can be structured into the network, while finding that cost advantage is not uniform across alternatives.

**Open boundary.** The study concerns postponement within a hardwood supply network. It does not test a resolved consumer project packet as the demand signal that moves a decoupling point onto dealer-held graded stock.

**Where it meets the sequence.** Both approaches delay irreversible form commitment. The sequence does so after spatial conditions, material choice, and permitted operations are sufficiently resolved.

**Research question at the seam.** Can a resolved project packet move a form-postponement point from a centralized network node to stock held at an independent dealer?

## C. Bounding the human work

A bounded cell does not remove human work. It moves it. Material identification and handling, setup, verification, exception recognition, quality control, labeling, staging, customer handoff, installation, maintenance, and refusal all remain.

### Woodwork Career Alliance of North America — *Woodwork Manufacturing Skill Standards* (2nd ed., 2016)

Not a research study. A standards framework, included because it is the working reference for describing woodworking capability in observable terms.

The standards define woodworking skills as measurable operations at stated performance levels. WCA's associated Passport — a separate instrument released alongside the second edition — records a worker's demonstrated competencies in a portable personal credential that travels with the worker rather than with an employer. Employers select the tool operations relevant to their own work.

**Open boundary.** WCA does not validate any system described in items 1–12, does not certify any cell, and does not establish that a credential qualifies a worker for a given material, machine, or site condition.

**Where it meets the sequence.** A packet can state the operations a project requires. A cell can state the operations it permits. A credential can state the operations a person has demonstrated.

**Research question at the seam.** Can observable, portable competencies describe who may execute, supervise, fulfill, or refuse bounded local work, without assuming a full custom-shop labor model?

## D. Conditions and place

These works are given briefly. Each establishes that a condition holds. None is a site where the question in this package is decided.

**Bumgardner and Buehlmann (2022)** — *A preliminary assessment of Industry 4.0 and digitized manufacturing in the North American woodworking industry.* Survey evidence from North American secondary woodworking firms, many of them small. Substantial recent investment in digitization is reported alongside persistent barriers in integration, skilled labor, and machine training, with only a minority of small firms reporting a vision for how digitization would affect the business. It does not examine whether a deliberately bounded capability is more adoptable than an integrated one.

**Legg, Dorfner, Leavengood and Hansen (2021)** — *Industry 4.0 implementation in US primary wood products industry.* The same picture one tier upstream: low robotics expertise, and skilled labor, facility age, and uncertain financial benefit reported as barriers. Included as corroboration across sectors rather than as separate evidence.

**Dissanayake et al. (2026)** — *Stakeholder perspectives on aligning sawmilling and prefabrication.* The authors report constraints in fragmentation, design timing, forecasting, inventory and moisture management, digital integration, distributor-mediated purchasing, interoperability, standards, and uneven skill pathways. Read against this package, the study suggests that physical capability alone does not resolve coordination problems when information and decisions remain poorly aligned — that reading is mine. The study examines the system level, not a single project arriving at an independent yard for acceptance or refusal.

**Greis, Cherukuri and Outeiro (2025)** — *Multi-agent systems for manufacturing digital twins.* Agency boundaries, responsibility allocation, and the relationship between autonomous agents and physical systems are identified as active engineering questions. It does not define an owner-held consumer record, local acceptance authority, or a declared envelope an agent may not widen.

**Hoppen et al. (2024)** — *Smart forestry: a forestry 4.0 approach to intelligent and fully integrated timber harvesting.* Information can persist across physical operations rather than disappearing at each handoff. The work begins at harvest and upstream wood flow; it does not begin with site conditions at a home or end with bounded fabrication against dealer-held stock.

**Parajuli and Bardon (2026)** — *Economic contribution of the forest sector in North Carolina, 2024.* The current official account of the regional setting into which any bounded test would enter. It measures existing sector activity and does not test whether independent dealers, yards, or shops can support a local increment of bounded processing.

## E. Lineage

None of this is new ground, and the document should not imply otherwise.

Postponement has a theoretical literature reaching back six decades. **Bucklin (1965)** framed the timing of form, time, and inventory commitment as a question of channel structure rather than equipment choice: differentiate late where the cost of being wrong exceeds the cost of holding generic form. **Skipworth and Harrison (2006)** carried that into plant-level manufacture, showing that postponing final form changes bills of material and process design, and that placing the customer-order decoupling point poorly undermines the intended benefit. **Hauslmayer and Gronalt (2008)** modeled configured variety in parquet flooring, examining how configuration can be accommodated within structured production rather than through unlimited process variation.

The idea that wood is too variable for computerized decision support has been under challenge for as long. **Kline, Conners and Araman (1997)**, working across Virginia Tech, the USDA Forest Service, and industry, reported automated hardwood scanning for grading features, with commercial systems emerging for sorting and rough-mill defect detection. American forest-products researchers have been working the boundary between material variability and machine decision-making for roughly thirty years.

## The seam

Taken together, the collected work establishes that several conditions are independently plausible: form commitment can be postponed; wood specification can move downstream under defined conditions; fabrication can move closer to material and place; execution can adapt to material that departs from its nominal model; digital information can persist across physical chains; manufacturing systems can be structured around declared authority and refusal; woodworking skill can be described through observable operations and portable records; and North Carolina holds substantial existing forest-sector activity.

The seam examined here is the territory where resolved individual demand meets ordinary graded stock already held at a dealer, and only as much process as that demand requires is added beside it.

---

## A reading of the two approaches

*The grouping, the weighting, and this reading are mine. It is not a finding, and it is not a characterization of how these research communities describe their own programs.*

Set against each other, the collected work approaches the same territory from two directions and does not meet in the middle.

One approach moves capability toward the material. Mallegol takes fabrication to an irregular stem; Adel senses deviation in ordinary lumber and corrects during the build; Nahmad Vazquez relocates the factory nearer the resource. In each case uncertainty is admitted into execution and handled there.

The other approaches from demand. Nicholls and Bumgardner follow customization pressure upstream toward suppliers; Bumgardner and Buehlmann find firms already digitizing and struggling with integration; Kühle moves the specification point later in the network; Dissanayake finds coordination, not capability, to be the binding constraint. In each case the question is how the chain reorganizes to absorb a more particular demand.

Note that this is a difference in method rather than geography. Adel is U.S. work that behaves like the first group; Nicholls and Bumgardner is U.S. work that defines the second. Any reading that maps the split onto continents does not survive the set.

The question this package raises sits between them. If demand can be resolved completely before material is committed, some of the uncertainty the first group handles at the machine, and some of the reorganization the second group anticipates in the chain, may not need to be handled at all — because it would already have been resolved, or refused.

That is a proposition, not a result. The strongest objection to it is in the set: Adel demonstrates that adapting during execution works, and works on exactly the ordinary dimensional lumber at issue here. Whether uncertainty is better resolved before commitment or sensed during execution is a real trade-off with a plausible case on both sides, and it is the question most worth putting to an institution capable of testing it.
