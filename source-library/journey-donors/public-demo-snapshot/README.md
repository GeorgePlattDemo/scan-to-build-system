# Scan to Build

**Single demonstration case**

3D Solutions LLC · Greensboro, NC  
U.S. patents 9,720,401 and 10,768,609

This folder exists so a reader can see the questions clearly.

It is not a product page. It is not a summary of the demo. Open the files after you know what is being asked.

## The place being tested

A physical object is purpose + material + some increment of process.

The analog end is already in the lumberyard: eyeballs, a tape, a pencil, and a saw the business already owns.

The high-capability end has already been shown in the field: take a robot to irregular wood, read the stem, cut to that shape.

The non-adjacent end is also familiar: send the fitting to a custom shop or a factory and let the wood travel.

What is being tested is none of those. It is a small increment of process standing next to stock the yard already paid to hold, move, and sell. Ordinary graded boards, not one-off stems. Purpose finished before the board moves. The yard loads what the packet lists and starts, or refuses the job.

That is the inquiry. "Middle" is only a name for that place.

## The questions

How much machine, and in what form, does that adjacent increment have to be?

Three answers are in bounds:

1. A resolved packet plus the saws already on the floor.
2. A small two-station cell with a fixed tool set. The yard loads the listed stock and starts, or refuses the job.
3. Nowhere. The economics only work somewhere else: a custom shop, a factory, or a forest robot.

A negative answer counts. So does a finding that this architecture is the wrong increment. The question that remains is whether some adjacent increment still produces the object the user wanted, and leaves more of that object's value with the wood, the yard, and the people already there.

Under that sit three tighter questions:

- Does the user get a better outcome than "buy generic boards and adapt them later"?
- Does more of the wood already on the rack become useful?
- Does the yard get used as infrastructure, or does the work still leave town?

## Why those questions exist

In rural France, a 2026 field study took a capable machine to irregular chestnut that a mill would not take. Each stem was scanned. Its actual geometry drove the cut. Real objects came out. Process moved because the material would not sit still. That is a current-state frontier example on a thin resource class: irregular stems plus a nearby reason to make objects from them. It is offered as proof that adjacency at the high end already works, not as a template for a lumberyard.

> Mallegol, Rohart and Champroy, "The tree dictates the shape: reversing the industrial paradigm in rural areas," *Construction Robotics* 10, article 30 (11 July 2026).

In North Carolina the forest sector produced $25.4 billion in direct industry output and directly employed about 66,500 people in 2024. Across the sector’s total economic contribution, output rose while employment fell.

> Parajuli and Bardon, *Economic Contribution of the Forest Sector in North Carolina, 2024*, NC State Extension AG-844.

Independent yards already have stock, floor, forklifts, saws, and pickup. A lot of the differentiating cut is still pencil, straightedge, and a practiced eye. Material and infrastructure already sit still. Yards already spend on technology that helps quote, track, optimize, move, and deliver material. The open question is how much of it turns a customer purpose into a resolved part on that same floor.

Those are two working ends doing different jobs. The distance between them is not whether a machine can cut wood. It is whether a finished purpose can travel to stock that is already sitting, and how small the machine can stay when that is the move.

The French study also names why their method is hard to copy on an ordinary floor: the cost of programming one-off parts on variable geometry, and the lack of commercial software for that task. That is their transfer problem, stated by them.

This packet asks whether, for ordinary graded stock already on a rack, that cost can leave the yard.
